Summary

This project investigates whether a gender wage gap exists among individuals aged 25 to 64 with a bachelor’s degree or higher, using 2022 median annual earnings data from the U.S. Census Bureau. After cleaning the dataset, the analysis compared men’s and women’s median earnings across all degree majors and field categories. Descriptive statistics, histograms, boxplots, and category-level comparisons all showed that women earn less than men in every major represented. On average, women earn $67,651 compared to $86,022 for men—about 79% of men’s earnings—demonstrating a clear and measurable gender wage gap.

Higher-paying disciplines such as Science & Engineering and Business show the largest wage gaps, while lower-paying fields like Education and Arts/Humanities/Other display smaller but still persistent disparities. A correlation of –0.56 between total earnings and the wage gap confirms that disparities increase as the salary level of a major rises. The dataset has limitations, including lack of information on work experience, hours worked, and whether individuals work in their field; yet, the evidence strongly indicates that a gender wage gap exists in 2022. These results provides a picture of the wage inequality at that time, while acknowledging that trends may have shifted since.

Purpose

The goal of this project is to determine whether a gender wage gap exists across fields of bachelor’s degree and whether pay level relates to the size of this gap. Using U.S. Census earnings data from 2022, this analysis compares men’s and women’s median annual earnings by field of study and tests whether higher or lower paying fields show more wage inequality.

Data

The dataset used for this project is titled “Median Annual Earnings for Field of Bachelor’s Degree for First Major by Sex in 2022,” was collected and published by the U.S. Census Bureau (2022).It contains median annual earnings estimates for adults aged 25 to 64 whose highest level of education is a bachelor’s degree or higher. The data is organized into a table with the following major columns: Field of Degree, Total Population, Men, Women, and Women’s Earnings as a Percentage of Men’s Earnings. Overall the original data had 44 rows by 9 columns.

The Field of Degree column includes four subrows that group related degree types. The four main field categories are:

  • Science and Engineering Degrees
  • Business Degrees
  • Education Degrees
  • Arts, Humanities, and Other Degrees

These section headers serve as section headers and aren’t actual observations. Aside from the category name, the remaining columns in these rows contain empty cells or NA values. All actual data observations appear below these headings, showing specific degree majors within each category.

Under Science and Engineering Degrees, the dataset includes the following majors: Computer Science, Engineering, Civil Engineering, Electrical Engineering, Mechanical Engineering, Mathematics, Biology, Chemistry, Psychology, Economics,Political Science, Sociology, Nursing, and Other Science and Engineering Degrees. Under Business Degrees, the dataset lists: General Business, Accounting, Business Management & Administration, Marketing, Finance, and Other Business Degrees. Under Education Degrees, the dataset lists General Education, Elementary Education, and Other Education Degrees. Finally, under Arts, Humanities, and Other Degrees, the majors include: Communications, English Literature & Languages, Liberal Arts, History, Fine Arts, Commercial Art & Graphic Design, Family & Consumer Sciences, Physical Fitness/Parks/Recreation/Leisure, Criminal Justice and Fire Protection, Social Work, and Other Degrees.

Under the remaining major columns Total Population, Men, Women each contain two subcolumns: Estimate and MOE. The Estimate subcolumn reports the median annual earnings for that group in U.S. dollars. The corresponding MOE value (Margin of Error) quantifies the uncertainty of the estimate, indicating how much the sample value may differ from the true population value. The final major column, Women’s Earnings as a Percentage of Men’s Earnings, also contains an Estimate and MOE pair. In this case, the estimate represents the percentage of men’s median earnings that women earn for a given degree, and its margin of error reflects sampling uncertainty in percentage units rather than dollars.

Data Cleaning

The dataset contained empty header rows and subrows from the original Excel formatting, which created NA values when imported. These were removed by using skip = 4 in read_excel(), from the readxl library, to bypass non-data rows. The columns were cleaned and standardized by manually renaming them using colnames(df_1) <- c(…) so that only valid variables with complete data were included in the analysis.

The only other missing or NA values in the dataset appeared in the category header rows (the subrows under each Field of Degree). These rows did not contain actual observations, so they were removed entirely during data cleaning to ensure that only valid degree majors remained in the analysis. Additionally, the descriptive footer rows at the bottom of the spreadsheet contained Census notes, source information, and disclosure statements. Those were also removed, as they did not contain any usable observations or numerical data for analysis.

Data Adjustments

A new variable was created to directly compare earnings between men and women. A column named WM_Gap was added, calculated as Women_Est − Men_Est. WM_Gap represents the gender wage gap for each degree major. This approach was chosen, over using the absolute value, because an initial inspection of the dataset revealed that women’s median annual earnings were never higher than men’s for any bachelor’s degree major. By subtracting women’s earnings from men’s earnings, the resulting values remain negative. Using absolute values would leave the gap’s direction up to interpretation; whereas, using negative values allows the analysis to explicitly reflect that women earn less across all observed fields. Overall the cleaned data’s dimensions was 34 rows by 10 columns.

[1] -0.563605

The correlation of –0.563605 indicates a moderately strong negative relationship between field earnings and the gender wage gap.A more negative (lesser) value represents a larger gap because the wage gap is defined as Women’s Earnings minus Men’s Earnings. Thus, the negative correlation shows that as the median earnings of a degree field increase, the gender wage gap becomes larger, with women earning increasingly less relative to men in higher paying fields.

Although this correlation does not imply causation, it does confirm that the wage gap is not random; rather, it follows a trend regarding pay level. Other factors,not considered by the Equal Pay Act of 1963, may be contributing to the unequal distribution of earnings.Another possibility is the data collection technique’s failure to establish more control variables between individuals.

Results

The descriptive statistics, visualizations, and correlation analysis consistently reveal that the gender wage gap not only exists across all bachelor’s degree majors and fields but grows larger as the salary of the major increases. The gap appears most noticeable in the highest-earning areas such as Science & Engineering and Business, where women earn between 74%–79% of men’s salaries. In contrast, lower-earning fields such as Education and Arts/Humanities show smaller gaps, with women earning 80%–82% of men’s earnings.Overall educated women received approximately 79% of what their men counterparts earned in 2022.

A correlation of –0.56 between total median earnings and the wage gap further demonstrates that disparities increase as the earning potential of a major rises. Together, these findings provide strong evidence that the gender wage gap is both real and systematically linked to the salary level of the field. Nevertheless, there are limitations that restrict the strength of these conclusions.

Limitations

Although the dataset provides valuable information about gender differences in median annual earnings by field of bachelor’s degree, there several limitations. First, the dataset does not include years of work experience, even though experience significantly affects earnings and may differ between men and women.The method of data collection is also unknown- whether the earnings were self-reported, employer-reported, or obtained through government records such as tax records or social security. The dataset also lacks information on hours worked, meaning differences between full-time and part-time employment cannot be accounted for. Furthermore, only two gender categories are reported, excluding non-binary or other gender identities. The dataset records only the first bachelor’s degree major, even though many individuals pursue graduate degrees or additional credentials that influence salary outcomes.The dataset reflects earnings from 2022, it represents only a single point in time; wage patterns may have shifted since then, meaning the gender gaps observed here could have either narrowed or widened in more recent years. Finally, the data assume that individuals are employed in fields related to their degree, which is not always the case. For example, an art major may be working in education or business.T These limitations should be considered carefully, as they represent factors outside the data’s control that may contribute to the wage differences observed.
